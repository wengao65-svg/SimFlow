# Results

Draft from known trends, definitions, and uncertainties; publication-quality
figures need not be finished. Organize Results by what the evidence establishes
about the scientific question, not by the order calculations passed review.

## Build the Argument

For the available results, identify the observation, the interpretation it
supports, and the unresolved question it leaves. This is a planning aid, not a
required visible table or a seven-item checklist for every subsection.

Choose an order based on dependencies. Examples include a structural observation
that motivates a lifetime measurement, competing interpretations that motivate
a control, a theoretical approximation that motivates a limiting-case test, or
an unresolved performance gap that motivates a method extension. Independent
results may instead provide complementary views of the same question; do not
invent a causal chain to connect them.

Framework-first, phenomenon-first, theory-first, process chronology, and combined
Results and Discussion can all be appropriate. A method sequence is scientific
when each step addresses an actual problem, not merely a completed work item.

## Paragraphs and Headings

Let a paragraph perform the argumentative work needed locally: describe a
phenomenon, quantify a contrast, introduce a necessary definition, assess an
alternative, or explain a consequence. These roles may span several paragraphs.
There is no mandatory first-sentence claim, four-sentence formula, or requirement
to include every role in every paragraph.

An opening can state an observation, ask a question, define an unfamiliar
quantity, or recall a relevant prior finding. Prefer scientific subjects such as
coordination populations, interfacial structure, electronic response, or estimator
variance over repeated "we calculated", "we checked", and "we accepted" openers.
First-person wording remains appropriate when the action itself needs explanation.

Use headings that identify the local question, phenomenon, or result. Concise
topic headings are legitimate; not every heading needs to assert a mechanism.
Avoid excessive subdivisions that turn each diagnostic into a separate result.

## Connect Evidence to Interpretation

- State the informative trend or contrast, with conditions and quantitative
  magnitude when relevant. Do not narrate every point or force a number into
  a qualitative structural observation.
- Explain why the chosen observable bears on the question. A descriptor is
  not a mechanism simply because it changes with the outcome.
- Describe what a comparison discriminates. Agreement with a reference may
  support accuracy; an intervention or controlled comparison may constrain an
  explanation. Neither automatically establishes broad transferability or causation.
- Distinguish compatible explanations from excluded ones. If controls cannot
  separate them, retain that ambiguity as part of the result.
- Link the next analysis to the remaining scientific issue, not to a figure
  number or a completed validation stage. Not every paragraph needs a transition.

Structural pairing can raise a question about molecular persistence, motivating
a lifetime analysis. In a method study, remaining generalization error can
motivate fine-tuning, and computational cost can motivate compression. These
are possible scientific dependencies, not facts to assume for the current work.

## Integrate Validation and Limits

Review evidence before drafting, but choose its presentation by interpretive
importance. A sensitivity test belongs next to the result if it changes the
reader's interpretation. Routine settings and supporting diagnostics can be
summarized and referenced to Methods or SI. Validation can also be the central
result in method or reliability papers.

One figure can both test a model and reveal physics. Do not force separate
validation and discovery figures, or require Fig. 1 to be a workflow and Fig. 2
to prove validity. Several figures can address one question from different angles.

Replace acceptance labels with the underlying finding only when that finding is
available. "Accepted free energies" does not specify the population ratio,
reference state, uncertainty, or sampling scope. If these are missing, ask for
them or narrow the text; never infer them from a passed audit.

State limitations in terms of the inference they affect. An equilibrium projected
free-energy profile does not alone establish a kinetic barrier. Explain that
boundary where the profile is interpreted; do not repeat a generic "not global,
not kinetic" disclaimer throughout unrelated results. Retain material exclusions
and negative results, including their effect on the comparison domain.

## Revision Check

Read the scientific subjects and paragraph transitions without the figure calls.
Can the reader follow what was learned and why the next analysis matters? Then
check the prose against the evidence: numbers, state definitions, reference
levels, uncertainty, exclusions, and causal strength must survive the revision.
Do not make the narrative stronger than the data to obtain a smoother ending.
