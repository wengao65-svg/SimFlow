# Reliability and Statistics Contributions

Use when estimator behavior, uncertainty, sampling, or reproducibility is itself
the scientific contribution. Here a criterion or procedure may legitimately be
the main result; that does not make it a default style for physical manuscripts.

## Explain the Error, Not Just the Verdict

Identify the quantity, the error source, and why existing estimation or reporting
can mislead. Connect statistical assumptions to observable consequences. For
example, a precise fitted slope does not alone establish adequate trajectory
sampling; an event-count relation can explain how sampling variance behaves.

A useful argument may develop an estimator, demonstrate its behavior, derive
uncertainty dependence, and translate this into usable guidance. Another may
start from a puzzling spread of results. Choose the order that explains the
problem; no fixed figure sequence or checklist figure is required.

## Match Evidence to the Statistical Claim

Define estimators and uncertainty measures, independence or correlation
assumptions, applicable regimes, and reference comparisons. Use repeated runs,
block analysis, analytic results, or long references as appropriate to the claim.
Distinguish fit uncertainty, sampling variance, systematic error, and model error.
Explain propagation into quantities such as activation energies where relevant.

Give numerical thresholds only when established. Conditional relations or
bounds can be useful contributions without a universal pass/fail cutoff. Do not
invent a minimum event count or insist on an impractical simulation length to
complete a template.

## Discussion

Practical or numbered recommendations are appropriate when they follow from the
analysis and retain its assumptions. The AIMD diffusivity-variance paper is an
example of this legitimate form. A physical paper that merely uses such an
estimator should report its findings and relevant uncertainty, not inherit a
procedure-centered Discussion.
